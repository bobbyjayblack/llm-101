# [BOOK] Yoav Goldberg — A Primer on Neural Network Models for Natural Language Processing

## Identification and course link

- Author: Yoav Goldberg.
- Version: author manuscript, draft dated October 5, 2015. This is a 76-page tutorial/manuscript, rather than the separately published 2017 book *Neural Network Methods for Natural Language Processing*.
- Official course link: Stanford CS224N Winter 2025 lists this exact title as a useful reference and links to the [author PDF](https://u.cs.biu.ac.il/~yogo/nnlp.pdf). The [arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.00726) identifies the same work.
- Evidence tier: **full-text review**. The complete public PDF was read at the section level. Page numbers refer to the PDF's printed numbering where visible.
- Status in CS224N: useful reference; the archived course page says no reference book is required. The linked Primer is the assigned reference corpus for this dossier; the 2017 Goldberg book is a distinct, supplementary publication.

## Table of contents and section-by-section review

The Primer is organized as twelve numbered technical sections, followed by references. Its stated scope is neural methods for NLP: input encoding, feed-forward, convolutional, recurrent and recursive networks, and computation graphs for automatic differentiation. It explicitly leaves attention, efficient large-vocabulary methods, caching, comprehensive language modeling and machine translation, multimodal models, and most unsupervised autoencoder methods outside its treatment.

1. **Introduction** (starts PDF p. 1) — The tutorial motivates the move from sparse linear NLP models to nonlinear networks over dense inputs and sets a unified notation. It is useful for LLM 101's transition from baselines to learned representations, especially lesson 2, “How language becomes an answer,” and lesson 3, “Data and evaluation.” The historical framing is from 2015 and should not be presented as a current account of decoder-only LLMs.
2. **Neural Network Architectures** (starts PDF p. 2) — This section distinguishes feed-forward networks from recurrent and recursive networks, explains what kinds of structure each preserves, and frames NLP prediction as mapping structured input to outputs. It gives a compact architecture taxonomy that can strengthen lesson 13, “Attention and transformer architecture,” by making the Transformer’s design choice explicit as a replacement for recurrence. The section discusses classifiers and fixed-width encodings; it does not describe Transformers or attention.
3. **Feature Representation** (starts PDF p. 4) — The section contrasts one-hot/sparse features with learned dense embeddings, including concatenation, summation/CBOW-style composition, feature sharing, and the embedding matrix lookup view. This is directly relevant to lesson 6, “Vectors, tensors, and a Python refresher,” and to the missing tokenization/embedding bridge before lesson 13. A useful teaching addition is to show how the same vocabulary item can have different parameters when feature roles are separated, then compare static word vectors with contextual token states.
4. **Feed-forward Neural Networks** (starts PDF p. 10) — Goldberg develops multilayer perceptrons, affine layers, nonlinearities (sigmoid, tanh, hard tanh, ReLU), output transformations such as softmax, embedding layers, and losses including hinge, log, cross-entropy, and ranking losses. The equations and examples provide a full-text foundation for lessons 7–9 and the existing “Check a gradient and a step” lab. They also expose a gap: LLM 101 should show one concrete vocabulary prediction with logits, softmax, cross-entropy, and a gradient update rather than leaving these as separate concepts.
5. **Word Embeddings** (starts PDF p. 19) — The section covers random initialization, supervised task-specific pretraining, unsupervised pretraining, training objectives, and the choice of contexts. It discusses window, sentence/document, syntactic, multilingual, character-based, and subword contexts, with word2vec/GloVe-style objectives as historical examples. This supports a new small embedding experiment alongside lesson 12, “Train a tiny language model,” but the dated treatment should be paired with a modern subword-tokenizer and contextual-embedding explanation.
6. **Training Neural Networks** (starts PDF p. 25) — Subsections are **6.1 Stochastic Gradient Training**, **6.2 The Computation Graph Abstraction**, **6.3 Optimization Issues**, and **6.4 Regularization**. The full text gives SGD and minibatch algorithms, reverse-mode differentiation/backpropagation, forward and backward graph passes, initialization, vanishing/exploding gradients, shuffling, learning-rate selection, minibatches, L2 regularization, dropout, and multi-task/cascading context. It is a strong source for lessons 7–9 and 14, and explains why the current gradient and tiny-network labs should expose intermediate tensors and reproducible seeds. It predates AdamW, modern normalization practice, mixed precision, and distributed training details in LLM 101's later units.
7. **Cascading and Multi-task Learning** (starts PDF p. 35) — Goldberg shows how component networks can be composed, share parameters, and receive gradients from multiple losses, including task-specific pretraining and pipeline/cascade designs. This maps to lesson 17, “Preferences, rewards, and distillation,” and to the project's proposed evaluation and evidence components. A bounded example could compare a shared encoder with two small classification heads, while explicitly separating multi-task learning from a production agent.
8. **Structured Prediction** (starts PDF p. 37) — Subsections are **8.1 Greedy Structured Prediction** and **8.2 Search Based Structured Prediction**. The section explains local decisions, error propagation, search over valid structures, neural scoring functions, reranking, generalized perceptron/hinge objectives, and the computational cost of evaluating parts of a candidate structure. It is relevant to the extractive assistant capstone: sentence selection is a structured decision over evidence units, so lesson 28 can make candidate generation, scoring, and failure cases visible without turning the capstone into open-ended generation.
9. **Convolutional Layers** (starts PDF p. 41) — Subsections are **9.1 Basic Convolution + Pooling**, **9.2 Dynamic, Hierarchical and k-max Pooling**, and **9.3 Variations**. The full text works through one-dimensional text convolutions, local windows, max/average pooling, positional grouping, hierarchical pooling, k-max pooling, and tree/path variants. It supplies optional historical context for Unit 11, “Autoencoders, GANs, and diffusion,” but does not justify adding a CNN requirement to the LLM path. A small optional lab could visualize receptive fields and show what local patterns pooling retains or discards.
10. **Recurrent Neural Networks — Modeling Sequences and Stacks** (starts PDF p. 45) — Subsections include **10.1 The RNN Abstraction**, **10.2 RNN Training**, **10.3 Multi-layer (stacked) RNNs**, **10.4 BI-RNN**, **10.5 RNNs for Representing Stacks**, and **10.6 A Note on Reading the Literature**. Goldberg explains state updates, acceptor/encoder/transducer/encoder-decoder supervision, backpropagation through time and truncated unrolling, stacked and bidirectional RNNs, persistent stack representations, and the danger of ambiguous architecture descriptions. This provides a useful pre-Transformer contrast for lessons 12–14 and a concrete reason to teach causal attention as parallelizable sequence processing. The encoder-decoder discussion is historical and does not include attention.
11. **Concrete RNN Architectures** (starts PDF p. 55) — Subsections are **11.1 Simple RNN**, **11.2 Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)**, **11.3 Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)**, and **11.4 Other Variants**. The full text defines the recurrence functions and gating intuition, discusses vanishing gradients and state retention, and surveys alternatives. It is useful only as optional architecture history in LLM 101; a brief comparison exercise can ask learners to trace one token through an RNN state and then through a Transformer residual stream. It should not obscure that modern LLM 101 lessons focus on attention and decoder-only models.
12. **Recursive Neural Networks** (starts PDF p. 59) — Subsections are **12.1 Formal Definition** and **12.2 Extensions and Variations**. The section represents parse-tree nodes with recursively composed vectors, discusses label-aware composition and tree-shaped LSTM variants, and connects the computation to structured representations. It can enrich the project's multimodal/structured-data perspective, but it is outside the current bounded LLM capstone. If included, use a short tree-composition example and preserve the primary sequence-modeling path.

## Relevance and gaps for LLM 101

The Primer is most useful as a bridge between the existing numerical foundation and the Transformer unit. Its full-text treatment supports:

- an explicit sparse feature → embedding lookup → dense vector walkthrough in lesson 6;
- a single end-to-end logits/softmax/loss/backprop trace in lessons 7–9;
- a tokenizer/subword and context-window lab before the tiny language model in lesson 12;
- a historical RNN/CNN comparison before or alongside lesson 13; and
- a structured scoring example for extractive evidence selection in lesson 28.

The Primer cannot supply modern LLM coverage by itself. It predates self-attention, positional encodings, Transformer blocks, scaling laws, large-corpus filtering, instruction tuning, preference optimization, retrieval augmentation, KV caching, quantization, and current safety/evaluation practice. Those topics are covered elsewhere in the university-course research and should remain separate additions rather than being attributed to this source.
