## Syllabus distilled

The class studies the abilities and limitations of GPT-4, Gemini, and LLaMA through linguistics and cognitive science. It asks whether model behavior reflects human-like language reasoning, how prompts and architecture affect failures, and how deployment creates privacy, environmental, copyright, security, bias, and stereotype risks. The syllabus allows the class to shift between reading and group experimentation based on participant interests.

Expectations are active attendance/participation; one to two readings per week; occasional one-page-plus responses summarizing an argument and posing a question; one student-led discussion/presentation week; and a final paper. Group projects are allowed and require a group presentation. Students must disclose LLM use in their work. The syllabus does not publish a percentage grade breakdown.

The plan is intentionally adjustable. Topic 1 is a brief history of language technologies, NLP tasks, and models. Topic 2 connects modern NLP to linguistics/cognitive science through compositionality, binding/control, logical and pragmatic inference, dialect variation, evaluation/benchmarks/datasets, annotation, and behavioral experiment design. Topic 3 addresses harm, bias, stereotypes, toxicity, misinformation/disinformation, hallucinations, training-data ownership, annotator labor, and benchmark construction. Topic 4 covers current affairs such as claims that LLMs have “solved” linguistics, sentience/AGI, environmental cost, and real-world use.

## What LLM 101 can borrow

1. **Add a linguistics-and-behavior track.** Pair token/prediction mechanics with small tests of compositionality, ambiguity, pragmatic inference, dialect variation, and calibration. This makes “what the model does” observable rather than anthropomorphic.
2. **Give ethics operational tests.** Add compact labs for hallucination, toxicity/bias, training-data provenance, privacy leakage, and benchmark construction. For every test, record the prompt set, model, date, output variance, and mitigation cost.
3. **Model uncertainty in the syllabus itself.** MIT’s plan labels topics as adjustable. LLM 101 can mark stable foundations versus fast-changing examples and give learners a “last checked” date for product/model claims.
4. **Use response questions as audio interaction.** Turn each reading response into a short spoken prompt: state the paper’s claim, name one piece of evidence, and ask one unresolved question. This suits the project’s audio-first design and preserves learner agency.
5. **Teach benchmark construction before benchmark scores.** Have learners write a tiny, diverse evaluation set and discuss annotation disagreement before comparing model outputs. That directly supports the project’s extractive assistant and guards against overtrusting a single score.
