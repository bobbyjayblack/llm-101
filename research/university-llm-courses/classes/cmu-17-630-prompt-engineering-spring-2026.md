## Sequence, goals, prerequisites, and assessment

The course begins with a short history from embeddings through transformers and generative pretrained models. It then moves through in-context learning and instruction tuning, chain-of-thought and multi-stage prompting, tool use, RAG, code generation, evaluation metrics, self-consistency, calibration, agentic frameworks, alignment, and ethics. The official schedule also situates the techniques in application areas such as law, medicine, and software engineering.

The stated learning goals are to design prompt instructions/templates, decompose complex inference into stages, run experiments that compare prompt designs, and evaluate prompt-based systems for performance, security, and reliability. The PDF does not list a separate prerequisite section; its workload and use of cloud LLMs imply that students need enough programming and ML/NLP background to complete the exercises, but that is an inference rather than a published requirement.

The Spring 2026 syllabus assigns 30% individual assignments, 30% project, 15% quizzes, 15% final take-home open-book/open-notes exam, and 10% class participation. It specifies three homework themes— in-context learning, tool use, and RAG—plus three in-class recitation projects. The project begins with a mid-semester proposal and uses biweekly progress check-ins. The syllabus says students receive $50 in OpenAI credit for coursework; this is a historical term-specific detail, not a recommendation for LLM 101 to add paid services.
