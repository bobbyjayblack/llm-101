# Stanford CS224N — Natural Language Processing with Deep Learning

## Identification and status

- Institution: Stanford University, Department of Computer Science.
- Course number and name: CS224N, *Natural Language Processing with Deep Learning*.
- Offering researched: Winter 2025, archived official course site.
- Status: completed/archived offering; the landing page preserves links to this and earlier offerings. This is a direct LLM course within a broader neural NLP sequence, not a generic “AI with ChatGPT” survey.
- Instructors: Diyi Yang and Tatsunori Hashimoto. Course manager: John Cho.

## Sequence, goals, prerequisites, and assessment

The course begins with word vectors, backpropagation, dependency parsing, sequence models, RNNs, and attention. It then moves through Transformers, pretraining, post-training (RLHF, supervised fine-tuning, and DPO), parameter-efficient adaptation and prompting, evaluation, question answering, retrieval, and interpretability. The stated goal is to teach both deep-learning foundations for NLP and current LLM research, with students implementing models in PyTorch.

The official prerequisites are Python, college calculus and linear algebra, basic probability/statistics, and foundations of machine learning. The site says students can take the course without prior ML/deep-learning coursework, but that the course is easier with it.

Assessment in this offering is unusually implementation-heavy: four assignments are 48% total; the final project is 49%, split across proposal (8%), milestone (6%), poster (3%), and report (32%); participation is 3% and includes guest-lecture attendance/reactions, surveys, Ed participation, and karma. The default project implements parts of GPT-2 and evaluates it on three downstream tasks; custom projects are also allowed. Teams may be solo or up to three people.

## Book status

No book is required. The official page labels six texts as useful reference texts and lists two more as optional background for students without neural-network preparation. The exact public list is:

- Dan Jurafsky and James H. Martin, [*Speech and Language Processing*](../books/jurafsky-martin-speech-language-processing-3rd-edition.md) (3rd-edition draft, 2024 pre-release).
- Jacob Eisenstein, [*Natural Language Processing*](../books/eisenstein-introduction-to-natural-language-processing-2019.md) (MIT Press, 2019).
- Yoav Goldberg, *A Primer on Neural Network Models for Natural Language Processing* (the linked PDF identifies itself as a draft dated October 5, 2015; later arXiv/publication versions should be distinguished from this course link; see the [Primer dossier](../books/goldberg-a-primer-neural-network-models-nlp-2015.md)).
- Ian Goodfellow, Yoshua Bengio, and Aaron Courville, [*Deep Learning*](../books/goodfellow-bengio-courville-deep-learning-2016.md) (MIT Press, 2016).
- Delip Rao and Brian McMahan, [*Natural Language Processing with PyTorch*](../books/rao-mcmahan-natural-language-processing-with-pytorch-2019.md) (O’Reilly, 2019).
- Lewis Tunstall, Leandro von Werra, and Thomas Wolf, [*Natural Language Processing with Transformers*](../books/tunstall-von-werra-wolf-natural-language-processing-with-transformers-2022.md) (O’Reilly, 2022).
- Michael A. Nielsen, [*Neural Networks and Deep Learning*](../books/nielsen-neural-networks-and-deep-learning.md) (online book; optional background).
- Eugene Charniak, [*Introduction to Deep Learning*](../books/charniak-introduction-to-deep-learning-2019.md) (MIT Press, 2019; the course page’s older citation may show 2018; optional background).

Only the first title is included in this task’s completed book dossier at present; the remaining seven are separate handoffs because “useful” and “background” are not required reading and chapter-level review needs edition-specific source work. The course page also cites individual chapters and papers; those are readings, not additional books.

## Student course feedback (separate from instructor reviews)

1. **Stanford’s own course page, date not stated.** It quotes an anonymous “CS224N student” saying the course taught them to write machine-learning models. Provenance: official Stanford page, but the quote is undated and unaudited; treat it as one curated testimonial, not a representative evaluation. [Source](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs224n/).
2. **Jazon Jiao, enrolled Stanford student account (2021 course, two-part blog).** The writer describes taking CS224N at Stanford: the first half centered on lectures and assignments, while the latter half shifted toward guest lectures and a final project. The account says projects could become research papers and characterizes the course as demanding but research-connected. It is an individual retrospective from an earlier offering and is partly translated/editorialized, so use it as qualitative context. [Part 1](https://www.jazonjiao.com/blog/nlp-1.html), [Part 2](https://www.jazonjiao.com/blog/nlp-2.html).
3. **Reddit discussion, 15 December 2021.** A commenter identifying themself as a former TA said CS224N did not require CS229; another commenter described high workload around Assignment 4 and a lenient/no-exam grading experience. Provenance: public participant discussion; identities and representativeness cannot be verified, and it predates Winter 2025. [Thread](https://www.reddit.com/r/stanford/comments/rf5i4l/).
4. **Matthew Turk, student course reflection (2023).** Search-indexed text from the author’s personal reflection describes CS224N as memorable, with lectures that repeatedly introduced new ideas and a minBERT final project that was stressful at times but manageable. The page was intermittently unavailable during retrieval, so this is retained as a source lead rather than treated as independently verified detail. [Reflection](https://www.matthewturk.com/blog/2023/03/26/students-of-the-cubicle).

These reports are useful for workload, project intensity, and research exposure only. They do not establish a course-quality rating, and none is a controlled student-evaluation dataset.
